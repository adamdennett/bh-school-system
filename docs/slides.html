The situation

A system built for more children than it has

  • The city publishes 2,515 Year 7 places across its ten secondary schools.
  • The modelled cohort is 2,267 in 2026 and 1,835 by 2035 — those children are already in primary school.
  • By 2035 that is about 720 empty Year 7 places a year.
  • Nine of the ten schools have been spending their reserves. The money is a city problem, not only a Longhill problem.

Longhill

  • Takes about 88 children in 2026 and 71 in 2030, on 210 places.
  • About £0.8m a year short of break-even in 2030.
  • Sits at the far edge of the city, at Ovingdean.

What the evidence says

  • This is not mainly a school quality problem. Most of what separates schools’ headline results arrives with the children; the part a school can reach runs mostly through attendance.
  • Families choose on the headline score, which largely describes the intake — so choice is self-fulfilling.
  • Admissions criteria only bind at some schools. Several schools offer a place to every first-preference applicant.
  • Physical access is unequal, in the worst direction. 29 neighbourhoods can reach no school place within 30 minutes by walking and bus, and access and child poverty go together.
  • Concentrated disadvantage is not the harm it is assumed to be. Nationally, disadvantaged pupils do slightly better in schools with more of them, once absence and prior attainment are allowed for. Brighton Aldridge is the local example: its disadvantaged pupils leave with about 3 GCSE points more than comparable pupils elsewhere (four-year average; 5.7 in 2024-25).
  • Children from the most deprived neighbourhoods travel 4.0 minutes further each way than everyone else, under the map in force.

How the model works

From published data to options

Orange steps are the ones the simulator lets you change (⚙). Moving any control re-runs everything from the start — about a twentieth of a second. Every step, opened up.

A spatial interaction model

\[T_{ij} = A_i\, O_i\, W_j\, c_{ij}^{-\beta}\, e^{\gamma_h\, \text{in catchment}}\, C_j^{\delta}\]

  • \(T_{ij}\) — children from neighbourhood \(i\) choosing school \(j\)
  • \(O_i\) — children living in \(i\)
  • \(W_j\) — how attractive school \(j\) is
  • \(c_{ij}\) — walk-and-bus minutes; \(\beta\) how fast demand falls with them
  • \(\gamma_h\) — how strongly each catchment follows its map
  • \(C_j\) — rival schools nearby
  • \(A_i\) — makes every neighbourhood place all its children

The options

How to read an option

Compare in the same year. The cohort falls by a fifth by 2035. Read an option against doing nothing in that year.

A cap that is never reached does nothing. Cutting Longhill to 150 changes only the count of empty places.

Small segregation differences are not findings. Anything under about 0.01 is within what re-measuring moved it.

Some results lean on held assumptions. Journeys are measured directly; segregation after a move assumes a school keeps its draw on disadvantaged families at a new site.

29 options, each run for 2026, 2030 and 2035 on every measure at once. How they were chosen, and seven rules for reading them.

Access or mixing? No option does both

The evidence leans towards access. A more mixed intake is a weak lever on results, and for disadvantaged pupils the national evidence runs the other way: they do slightly better where they are concentrated in a school organised for them, as at Brighton Aldridge. The higher index counts against an option under the council’s aim, not on the evidence about results. Whether shorter journeys raise attendance is what the council’s own records could settle.

What this means for Longhill

A smaller Longhill at the top of Elm Grove

What it gains

  • The biggest cut in deprived children’s journeys of any option.
  • 4 of the 6 Whitehawk neighbourhoods back in Longhill’s catchment, as part of a redesign rather than a reversal.
  • More children for Longhill.

What it costs

  • A site, not costed here. Longhill still does not break even.
  • About 337 children a year in neighbourhoods that change catchment; longer journeys for Rottingdean, Saltdean and Woodingdean.
  • Intakes more concentrated: a cost under the council’s aim, not a measured cost to disadvantaged pupils’ results. The figure also assumes families follow a new map as closely as today’s.

Recommendations

What to do

  1. Lead with a smaller Longhill at the top of Elm Grove, with catchments redrawn round it — and test it properly, costs stated.
  2. If the site cannot be had, return Whitehawk to Longhill’s catchment on its own.
  3. Set Longhill at 150 on either site, and budget for the children it will get, not for 150.
  4. Ask Cardinal Newman (300), King’s (150) and Brighton Aldridge (150) to take fewer, and every school to share the fall in proportion by 2030.
  5. Trim Stringer, Varndean and Blatchington Mill only if Longhill stays at Ovingdean and only with a catchment redraw.
  6. Publish where priority 6’s places went.
  7. Ask the inequalities review to settle the measure — journeys or mixing — and whether journey length affects attendance, weighing the evidence that disadvantaged pupils do no worse where they are concentrated and well served.

Next steps

  • Cost the Elm Grove site, and re-run the move on the council’s own records before deciding.
  • Draw the Elm Grove catchment with Rottingdean, Saltdean and Woodingdean in the room, before rather than after.
  • Check the disadvantaged-intake calibration against a second year of Year 7 offers.
  • Release ten small tables the council already holds — applicants’ home areas, full preferences, allocations, the criterion each place was given under, offers to children outside the city, and the catchment preference table for each year — which would turn most of the ranges here into estimates.
  • Link journeys to attendance in the council’s records, for the inequalities review.

What this rests on

  • A model calibrated to published catchment totals, not individual applications.
  • Catchment attachment carried to redrawn maps at the strength families follow the maps they have — the assumption every redraw result depends on.
  • Finances as a five-year steady state; site and capital costs not included.
  • The academies and faith schools modelled as responding to demand, not setting their own numbers.
  • The direction of each finding is more reliable than its size.
