A technical guide for checking each element

Author

Adam Dennett, UCL Centre for Advanced Spatial Analysis

Published

September 16, 2026

Abstract

This document takes the model the simulator runs and opens it up one element at a time. Each section states what a term is, where its data comes from, exactly how it is computed, what it looks like for a worked example, the checks that can be run against it, and what it assumes. It is written to be audited: every number is produced by the code in this repository, and every step should be reproducible by hand.

1 What this document is

The simulator runs one model. Everything in the strategic view, and every scenario in it, is that model with different settings. This guide takes it apart in the order the model is written, so that each piece can be checked on its own.

\[ T_{ij}^{\,y} \;=\; A_i^{\,y}\, O_i^{\,y}\, W_j\, c_{ij}^{-\beta}\, e^{\gamma_{h(i)} \cdot \mathbb{1}[\,i \in \text{catchment}(j)\,]}\, C_j^{\delta}, \qquad A_i^{\,y} = \frac{1}{\sum_k W_k\, c_{ik}^{-\beta}\, e^{\gamma_{h(i)} \mathbb{1}[\cdot]}\, C_k^{\delta}} \]

Symbol Meaning Section
\(T_{ij}\) children from neighbourhood \(i\) modelled as attending school \(j\) the interior of the matrix
\(O_i\) children in neighbourhood \(i\) entering Year 7 §2
\(W_j\) how attractive school \(j\) is, net of where it stands §3
\(c_{ij}\) routed walk-and-bus journey time from \(i\) to \(j\) §4
\(\beta\) distance decay §5
\(\gamma_h\) catchment term, fitted for each catchment \(h\) §6
\(C_j\), \(\delta\) competing destinations to follow
\(A_i\) balancing factor: every neighbourhood places all of its children to follow
\(y\) entry year, carried as a superscript §2.7

Two further stages are applied to the \(T_{ij}\) this produces: the admission-number ceiling with the council’s oversubscription priorities, and the re-offering of refused children. Each gets its own section after the terms above.

NoteThe year is the only dynamic element, and it enters in one place

Nothing in the equation carries a time subscript except \(O_i\). Journey times, attractiveness, catchment terms and admission numbers are all held at a single point in time, and the year chosen on the simulator’s slider changes only the size of each neighbourhood’s cohort (§2.7). Whether that is the right treatment — and what an explicit time dimension would have to carry — is a question this guide returns to once the static terms are set out.

The interior of that matrix is what the model produces. The margins are what it is given, or what it fits. \(O_i\) is the one margin that is a count of real people, and it is where this guide starts.

2 \(O_i\): the children in each neighbourhood

2.1 What it is

\(O_i\) is the number of children living in zone \(i\) who will enter Year 7 at a state secondary school in the year being modelled. The model is production-constrained on it, which means that whatever else happens,

\[\sum_j T_{ij} = O_i \quad \text{for every } i.\]

Every child is placed somewhere. Nothing is created and nothing is lost, which is why \(O_i\) has to be right before anything else can be.

2.2 What a zone is

A zone is an LSOA cut by catchment, keyed as LSOA | pre-2024 catchment | current catchment. The city has 195 zones drawn from 165 LSOAs.

The cut exists because the 2024 boundary change runs through LSOAs. Assigning a whole LSOA to whichever catchment contains its centroid would register only a fraction of the children the redraw actually moves. So every postcode in the ONS Postcode Directory is assigned a catchment on each of the two maps by point-in-polygon, and the LSOA is split accordingly. Carrying both maps in the key means the two regimes sit on identical geography and can be compared without rebuilding anything.

2.4 How \(O_i\) is built, step by step

The code is public/R/01_open_inputs.R in the private bundle, sections 1b and 1c. Its result travels to the simulator as data/open_inputs.rds.

Step 1 — ages become entry years. A child aged \(a\) in the mid-2022 estimates enters Year 7 in \(2022 + (11 - a)\). Ages 0 to 11 therefore give entry years 2022 to 2033, every one of them children ONS has already counted rather than births anyone has projected.

Step 2 — two extrapolated years. Children entering in 2034 and 2035 were not born when the mid-2022 estimate was taken. They are a linear continuation of the five most recent observed cohorts, 2029 to 2033, and are flagged as extrapolated wherever they appear.

Step 3 — resident children become state-sector demand. Not every resident child takes a state Year 7 place: some go to independent schools, some leave the city. A single constant carries that:

\[\text{state share} \;=\; \frac{\text{Year 7 offers made, 2024}}{\text{resident children, 2024 entry}} \;=\; 0.850\]

taken from the council’s published offer total against the ONS cohort of the same year.

Step 4 — a ward is divided between its zones. ONS publishes the age detail at ward level, so each ward’s children are shared out between the zones in it. The weight for a zone is its LSOA’s age-11 population share, divided between the zones that LSOA is cut into by their postcode counts:

\[u_i \;=\; \underbrace{p_{\ell(i)}}_{\text{LSOA age-11 share}} \times \underbrace{\frac{n_i}{\sum_{k \in \ell(i)} n_k}}_{\text{postcodes in this zone}}, \qquad O_i^{\,2026} \;=\; \text{ward children}_{w(i)} \times \frac{u_i}{\sum_{k \in w(i)} u_k} \times \text{state share}\]

where \(\ell(i)\) is the zone’s LSOA, \(w(i)\) its ward and \(n_i\) its postcode count. 195 of the 209 zones use the ONS age share; the 14 in the expansion area outside the city fall back to postcode counts alone.

NoteWhy not divide a ward by postcode count

Splitting a ward’s children by how many postcodes each LSOA holds assumes every postcode contains the same number of eleven-year-olds. Family housing, student housing and retirement housing differ enormously on that, and the difference runs along exactly the lines catchment boundaries follow. An earlier version of this work used postcode counts between LSOAs, and the deprivation contrast between the two halves of a paired catchment was almost entirely smoothed away. Postcode counts do the narrower job above — dividing one LSOA between its own fragments, where nothing finer is published.

ImportantCorrected: a split LSOA used to be counted twice

Until this version, each zone of a split LSOA inherited the whole LSOA’s age-11 share as its weight. Ward and city totals were unaffected, because the denominator grew too, but inside a ward a split LSOA took about 1.7 to 1.9 times the share its population implied and the unsplit LSOAs around it took correspondingly less. The splits sit exactly on the catchment boundaries, so the error was concentrated in the neighbourhoods these questions turn on — Whitehawk and Kemptown among them.

The weight is now divided between a split LSOA’s zones by their postcode counts, as in the formula above. The table below is the check that used to fail.

2.6.1 Where the city totals come from

Two different city-wide totals appear in this work, and they have different provenance. It is worth being explicit about which is which.

Offers actually made are the council’s own published figures. Its allocation factsheets are issued after each March allocation round, and the series from 2010 to 2026 was transcribed school by school in the earlier Brighton and Hove allocations analysis. This bundle does not retype them: public/R/01a_factsheet_panel.R re-extracts the year-by-year tables from that document, so every offer, preference and admission number here traces back to a published council factsheet.

Resident children are ONS, as set out in Section 2.3. They are a different quantity from offers: a resident child may go to an independent school, or out of the city.

The model’s city total is the first anchored on the second. The state-sector share of 0.850 is the council’s published 2024 offer total divided by the ONS resident cohort for the same entry year, so the model’s demand is ONS children scaled to the level the council’s own offers imply. It is not a free-floating estimate.

2.7.1 Which projection this is, and the one beside it

Two projections of the city’s Year 7 cohort appear in this work. They are built from different data and they are used for different things, so it is worth being plain about which drives the model.

The one the slider uses is the ONS estimate carried forward: the children counted in the mid-2022 small-area estimates, aged forward to their Year 7 entry year (§2.4, steps 1 and 2), and multiplied by the state-sector share. It is used because it is the only one that exists at ward level, and the model needs the geography as well as the total — \(O_i\) is a ward figure divided between zones.

The one beside it is built from children already in the city’s primary schools: Reception offers made seven years earlier, multiplied by the share of a Reception cohort that reappears as a Year 7 offer. That ratio is measured rather than assumed, from the council’s own published factsheets, and it has been strikingly steady — 88.4% across the five most recent cohorts, with a standard deviation of 1.6%. The method is set out in full in how our projection method works, and section 3 of the strategic view uses it as its headline projection because it rests on children already registered in a school rather than on a population estimate.

2.9 What to be careful about

  • The base is mid-2022. Migration into or out of the city since then is not in the counts, and a child who has moved between wards is still counted where they were.
  • One state-sector share, for every year and every neighbourhood. It is 0.850, from a single round. If independent-school take-up varies across the city — and it plainly does — then \(O_i\) is too high in some places and too low in others.
  • Ward-level age detail. Within a ward, the LSOA age-11 distribution does the work; how children are distributed inside an LSOA is not observed, so postcode counts stand in for it where a boundary cuts one.
  • Peacehaven and the expansion area are carried in the open bundle but excluded from the simulator’s zones. Peacehaven Community School is a destination in the model, not a source of demand.
  • No composition. \(O_i\) is a headcount. Which of those children are disadvantaged is a separate layer applied later, not part of this term.

3 \(W_j\): how attractive each school is

3.1 What it is

\(W_j\) is the pull of school \(j\) on a family, net of where the school stands. It is dimensionless, scaled so the city average is 1, and it enters the model multiplicatively: doubling \(W_j\) doubles the numerator of every flow into \(j\) before distance, catchment and competition have their say. It also enters the competing-destinations term, because a school’s rivals count for more when they are schools families want.

It is the hardest term in the model to pin down, because everything observable about a school is entangled with where it is. A school with high attainment may be sought after because it teaches well, or because it sits where better-off families already live.

Layer two — the fitted values the simulator uses. The full model does not take any of those five as given. \[W_j \;\leftarrow\; W_j \left(\frac{\text{target share}_j}{\text{modelled share}_j}\right)^{0.8}, \qquad \text{repeat until } \max_j |\text{modelled} - \text{target}| < 10^{-6}\]

This is the same device as the balancing factor \(A_i\), applied to the other margin of the matrix — \(A_i\) makes every neighbourhood place all its children, and this makes every school receive the share of first preferences it is observed to receive.

ImportantHow the model handles this, and what it cannot do

What it does. It fixes attractiveness at its recent value and gives you a slider to move it. Every scenario in the strategic view that says “a school half as attractive again” is that slider, and §3.6 converts a slider position into points of Attainment 8 so the size of the assumption is legible rather than abstract.

What it does not do. Attractiveness never responds to anything the model itself produces. A school whose roll falls does not become less wanted in the next year of the run; a school whose intake changes does not see its results, and therefore its reputation, move with it. The feedback loop the strategic view describes — a more disadvantaged intake, lower headline scores, fewer preferences, a more disadvantaged intake still — is outside this model. Where that loop is real, a run over ten years understates how far apart schools drift.

That is the clearest case in this document for making time explicit rather than implicit: \(O_i\) already carries a year, and the natural next step would be to let \(W_j\) carry one too.

3.4 Why there is no exponent on \(W_j\)

The general form of a production-constrained model carries a power on attractiveness as well as on cost:

\[T_{ij} \;=\; A_i\, O_i\, W_j^{\alpha}\, c_{ij}^{-\beta}\]

Here \(\alpha\) is absent, which is the same as fixing it at 1. That is a deliberate choice and it follows from what \(W_j\) is.

A fitted quantity does not need a parameter to scale it. \(W_j\) is not a proxy standing in for attractiveness; it is attractiveness, balanced until the model reproduces each school’s share of the city’s first preferences. An exponent on it would be a second free parameter doing the same job as the balancing, and the two would trade off against each other: any \(\alpha\) can be absorbed by re-balancing \(W\), because \((W^{1/\alpha})^{\alpha} = W\). Fitting both would be fitting the same thing twice, and the pair would not be identified.

It would be needed if \(W_j\) were a proxy. Suppose attractiveness were taken crudely as the number of pupils on roll, or as the admission number, or as Attainment 8 — all of which this work carries as alternative specifications (§3.2). None of those is pull; each is a measurable thing believed to move with it, and there is no reason the relationship should be one-for-one. A school twice the size is not necessarily twice as wanted. An exponent would then be exactly the right device: \(\alpha\) fitted to observed flows would say how strongly demand responds to the proxy, and \(\alpha = 1\) would be a finding rather than an assumption. In the classic retail application that is precisely what the exponent on floorspace does.

So the rule is: balance it, or scale it, but not both. This model balances. A version of it built on roll or on published attainment, which is what anyone without the council’s preference table would have to do, should carry an \(\alpha\) and fit it.

3.6 What the attractiveness slider does

Each school’s slider multiplies its fitted \(W_j\), from 0.25 to 4 times. Nothing else changes: the same journey times, the same catchment terms, the same admission numbers. Three details are worth knowing.

  • The multiplier is applied to the fitted value, not to weighted preferences, so a slider at 1 reproduces the model exactly.
  • The out-of-city schools are not touched by it. Priory, Peacehaven, Seahaven and Seaford Head carry their own fitted attractiveness and a decay on straight-line distance, and the sliders do not reach them.
  • CoMArt has no preferences, because it does not exist. When it is opened, it borrows the attractiveness of Brighton Aldridge as its starting value, and its slider moves from there.

3.7 Turning a multiplier into something meaningful

A slider position is not a quantity anyone outside a model can judge, so the app converts it to the one published number families appear to respond to. Across the ten city schools, the fitted attractiveness is log-linear in Attainment 8:

\[\log W_j = a + b \cdot \text{Attainment 8}_j, \qquad b = 0.1163, \quad R^2 = 0.62, \quad n = 10\]

so a point of Attainment 8 is worth about 12% more attractiveness, and a multiplier \(m\) inverts to \(\log(m) / b\) points. That is how the simulator answers “how good would this school have to be to fill?” — and it is an association, not a lever: Attainment 8 is itself largely a description of who a school admits.

3.9 What to be careful about

  • Ten observations. Every statement about why a school is attractive rests on ten schools. The balancing is an accounting identity across those ten; the Attainment 8 fit is a description of them, not evidence about causes.
  • Attractiveness and location are only partly separated. The balancing asks what pull each school needs given where it stands and how far families will travel, which is a real separation — but it inherits whatever \(\beta\) and \(\gamma_h\) are set to. If distance decay were weaker, the fitted \(W_j\) would be different.
  • The faith schools have no catchment, so nothing in the model restricts who may choose them. Their fitted attractiveness absorbs the religious criteria that really ration those places, which is why they carry high values.
  • Peacehaven’s preferences are not published by the council, so both preference measures are filled at the lowest observed rate in the city. That is an assumption, and it flatters Longhill: a weak Peacehaven keeps eastern children in the city.
  • \(W_j\) does not move with time. A school that improves, or a new building, is a slider position rather than anything the model generates. Whether attractiveness should respond to a school’s roll, its intake or its results is exactly the dynamic question §2.7 flags.

3.11 What it is

\(c_{ij}\) is the generalised cost of getting from neighbourhood \(i\) to school \(j\): minutes of walking and bus, door to door, on a weekday morning. It is not a straight-line distance, and it is not a car journey. It enters the model as \(c_{ij}^{-\beta}\), so it is the term that makes a school ten minutes away far more likely to be chosen than one an hour away — and the exponent \(\beta\) that governs how fast that falls away is its own section, to follow.

3.13 From postcodes to zones

The matrix is routed postcode to school. A zone’s cost is the mean over the live postcodes in it:

\[c_{ij} \;=\; \frac{1}{|P_i|}\sum_{p \in P_i} c_{pj}\]

where \(P_i\) is the set of postcodes in zone \(i\). Because a zone is an LSOA cut by catchment (§2.2), the two halves of a split LSOA get their own journey times — which is the point of cutting them.

3.14 Two corrections the raw routing needs

Walking is always available. Where the bus network is awkward the router returns a poor itinerary: about a fifth of pairs came back slower than simply walking the distance, in one case 99 minutes for 4 km. That is a property of the router, not of the city, so every journey is capped at the time it would take to walk:

\[c_{ij} \;=\; \min\!\left(c^{\text{routed}}_{ij},\; \frac{d_{ij} \times 1.3}{4.35 \text{ km/h}} \times 60\right)\]

The walking pace comes from a systematic review of active school travel: children’s mean walking trip is 1,030 m in 14.2 minutes, or 4.35 km/h, and the circuity factor of 1.3 turns straight-line distance into street distance. The cap binds on 19% of pairs. It is a ceiling on modelled time, not a claim that children walk that far — the same review finds they rarely walk beyond 1.8 km.

A distance model fills the gaps. Where the matrix has no routed value at all, a fitted model of time on straight-line distance stands in. It reaches 0.1% of pairs in the city, so almost everything here is routed rather than predicted.

3.19 What to be careful about

  • One departure, one day. Every journey is a single weekday morning in the timetable as it stood when the matrix was built. Term-time variation, a missed connection and a cancelled bus are all outside it.
  • A mean over postcodes. A zone’s cost is the average of the postcodes in it, so a neighbourhood with one badly connected corner shows as slightly worse rather than showing both.
  • No cars, no lifts, no walking buses. The model prices the journey a child could make on foot and by bus. A family with a car makes a different journey, and that is one reason the east’s figures should be read as a ceiling on difficulty rather than a description of it.

  • The cap is generous at distance. A 6 km walk is not a real school journey, but capping at walking time is deliberately crude: it removes router artefacts without inventing a bus that does not run.

4 \(\beta\): how sharply distance deters

4.1 What it is

\(\beta\) is the exponent on journey time. It converts the minutes of §4 into a pull:

\[\text{pull of school } j \text{ on neighbourhood } i \;\propto\; c_{ij}^{-\beta}\]

A larger \(\beta\) means families discount distance more sharply. It is a single number for the whole city, fixed at 1.7, and it is the same in every run of the simulator: no slider moves it.

The form matters as much as the value. This is a power decay, not an exponential one: doubling the journey divides the pull by \(2^{1.7} \approx 3.2\), whatever the starting point. That is the standard form in spatial interaction modelling, and it behaves sensibly over the range of journeys a city this size contains.

4.2 Where the value comes from, and what it is not

It is a judgement, not an estimate. Fitting \(\beta\) properly needs observed flows — how many children actually travel from each neighbourhood to each school — and the council has not released them. A catchment-level preference table identifies the catchment terms (§6, to follow) but not distance decay, because within a catchment every family is at a different distance and the table does not say who is where.

So 1.7 is a central value chosen for a system of this kind: journeys are short, the alternatives sit close together, and it is squarely inside the range this literature reports for school choice. The open model does not rely on it being right. It runs everything across 18 values from 1.5 to 3.2 and reports which conclusions survive the whole range.

NoteWhat would pin it down

A Poisson regression of flows on log journey time with an origin fixed effect returns \(\beta\) with a standard error, from records the admissions process already produces. It is half a day’s work on data the council holds. Every band in the open model that comes from sweeping \(\beta\) would become a number with an interval around it.

4.5 A second decay, on a different scale

The four schools outside the city do not use \(\beta\) at all. They are chosen on straight-line kilometres with a decay of their own, fitted to the published 2024 destinations:

\[\text{pull of an out-of-city school} \;=\; W^{\text{ext}}_j \; d_{ij}^{-8.0}\]

8.0 is far steeper than 1.7, and the two are not comparable: this one is on kilometres rather than minutes, and it is doing a different job. It is absorbing everything that stops a Brighton family choosing a school in another authority — the admissions arrangements of a different council, the absence of a school bus, the sheer unfamiliarity — and compressing it into the one dimension the fit has. It reproduces the published pattern, which is a low bar for four schools and one year of data, and it should not be read as a behavioural estimate at all.

4.7 What to be careful about

  • One exponent for the whole city. Families differ in how far they will travel, and the literature is consistent that the willingness varies with income and with whether a car is available.   • Everything else is fitted given this value. The attractiveness balancing (§3) and the catchment terms (§6) are estimated with \(\beta\) held at 1.7.   • Time, not distance, and not cost. A journey that is quick but requires two buses and a fare is treated as a short journey.
  • The floor at short journeys. A power decay rises steeply as time approaches zero, so the very shortest journeys carry a lot of weight. The routed minimum in the table is 3 minutes, which keeps it in bounds.

  3. Should the out-of-city decay be re-fitted once more than one year of destination data is available, or replaced by a proper choice model for schools in another authority? # \(\gamma_h\): how much living in the catchment counts {#sec-gamma}

4.9 What it is

\(\gamma_h\) is the bonus a school gets for being in the family’s own catchment. It sits in the exponent, so it multiplies a school’s pull:

\[\text{pull} \;\propto\; W_j\, c_{ij}^{-\beta} \times \begin{cases} e^{\gamma_{h(i)}} & \text{if } j \text{ is a school of } i\text{'s catchment}\\ 1 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\]

The subscript matters: \(\gamma\) is indexed by \(h(i)\), the catchment the family lives in, not by the school. Stringer / Varndean families are modelled as following their map more closely than Brighton Aldridge’s families follow theirs, and that is a statement about families rather than about schools.

ImportantThis is about what families name, not about who gets admitted

Admission priority is a separate stage of the model (§8, to follow): when a school is oversubscribed the council’s criteria decide who is offered a place, and living in the catchment is one of them. \(\gamma_h\) is doing something different and earlier. It says how much more likely a family is to want a school because it is the one their map points them to — through habit, siblings, the school bus, or simply reading the catchment as a statement about where their child belongs.

The distinction is why the strategic view can find that catchment priority allocates nothing at an undersubscribed school while the catchment still matters a great deal there: nobody is turned away, but the map still shapes what families ask for.

4.11 How they are fitted

The council’s evidence to the Schools Adjudicator includes a catchment-level preference table: how many first, second and third preferences each catchment’s children gave each school, pooled over 3 rounds. That is a flow matrix — coarse, but a matrix — and it is enough to fit one term per catchment.

The loop is:

  1. Take the current catchment terms and balance \(W_j\) so the model reproduces each school’s share of the city’s first preferences (§3).
  2. For one catchment, try every \(\gamma\) on a grid from 0 to 4 in steps of 0.1, re-balancing \(W_j\) at each step, and keep the value that minimises the Poisson deviance between the modelled and observed shares in that table.
  3. Repeat for each catchment, then sweep again until nothing moves.

The deviance is the right loss here because the cells are counts of preferences: it weights a cell by how many families it contains, so a catchment that gives 400 first preferences counts for more than one that gives 40.

4.16 What to be careful about

WarningThe assumption every redraw result depends on

The terms are fitted on the map families have lived with, and then carried unchanged to any other map. A redraw in the simulator moves which schools count as in catchment; it does not change how strongly a family follows the line.

That is the single most consequential assumption in this work. If families follow a new boundary less closely than the one they know — which the strategic view finds some evidence for in what happened after 2024 — then every redraw result here is too optimistic about how many children the redraw moves. The strategic view reports the halved-term version of the relocation runs for exactly this reason.

  • The term is fitted on the pre-2024 map, because that is the map the preference table covers.
  • It bundles several mechanisms. Siblings already at the school, the free bus, habit, and the sense that the catchment school is the school all end up in one number, because the table cannot separate them.
  • It is a home-catchment term, not a school term. A school in two catchments — none exist today, but CoMArt opening would create one — takes each catchment’s own strength.
  • Paired catchments are a single object here. A Stringer / Varndean family gets the bonus on both schools, and which of the two they prefer is left to \(W_j\), distance, and the exclusivity populations.

4.17 Questions this raises

  1. Should the term decay when a boundary changes — a family newly placed in a catchment following it less closely than one that has been in it for years?
  2. Should it vary with how many schools a catchment contains, rather than being one number per catchment?
  3. Would first preferences by small area, before and after the 2024 change, let the normative half of this be separated from the procedural half?

# What this document is

The simulator runs one model. Everything in the strategic view, and every
scenario in it, is that model with different settings. This guide takes
it apart in the order the model is written, so that each piece can be
checked on its own.

$$
T_{ij}^{\,y} \;=\; A_i^{\,y}\, O_i^{\,y}\, W_j\, c_{ij}^{-\beta}\,
e^{\gamma_{h(i)} \cdot \mathbb{1}[\,i \in \text{catchment}(j)\,]}\, C_j^{\delta},
\qquad
A_i^{\,y} = \frac{1}{\sum_k W_k\, c_{ik}^{-\beta}\, e^{\gamma_{h(i)} \mathbb{1}[\cdot]}\, C_k^{\delta}}
$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Section |
|:--|:--|:--|
| $T_{ij}$ | children from neighbourhood $i$ modelled as attending school $j$ | the interior of the matrix |
| $O_i$ | children in neighbourhood $i$ entering Year 7 | §2 |
| $W_j$ | how attractive school $j$ is, net of where it stands | §3 |
| $c_{ij}$ | routed walk-and-bus journey time from $i$ to $j$ | §4 |
| $\beta$ | distance decay | §5 |
| $\gamma_h$ | catchment term, fitted for each catchment $h$ | §6 |
| $C_j$, $\delta$ | competing destinations | to follow |
| $A_i$ | balancing factor: every neighbourhood places all of its children | to follow |
| $y$ | entry year, carried as a superscript | §2.7 |

Two further stages are applied to the $T_{ij}$ this produces: the
admission-number ceiling with the council's oversubscription priorities,
and the re-offering of refused children. Each gets its own section after
the terms above.

changes only the **size** of each neighbourhood's cohort (§2.7). Whether

# $O_i$: the children in each neighbourhood {#sec-oi}

## What it is

$O_i$ is the number of children living in zone $i$ who will enter Year 7
at a **state** secondary school in the year being modelled. The model is
production-constrained on it, which means that whatever else happens,

$$\sum_j T_{ij} = O_i \quad \text{for every } i.$$

Every child is placed somewhere. Nothing is created and nothing is lost,
which is why $O_i$ has to be right before anything else can be.

## What a zone is

A zone is **an LSOA cut by catchment**, keyed as
`LSOA | pre-2024 catchment | current catchment`. The city has
`r fmt_n(nrow(zc))` zones drawn from `r fmt_n(n_distinct(zc$lsoa))`
LSOAs.

The cut exists because the 2024 boundary change runs *through* LSOAs.
Assigning a whole LSOA to whichever catchment contains its centroid would
register only a fraction of the children the redraw actually moves. So
every postcode in the ONS Postcode Directory is assigned a catchment on
each of the two maps by point-in-polygon, and the LSOA is split
accordingly. Carrying **both** maps in the key means the two regimes sit
on identical geography and can be compared without rebuilding anything.

## How $O_i$ is built, step by step

The code is `public/R/01_open_inputs.R` in the private bundle, sections
1b and 1c. Its result travels to the simulator as `data/open_inputs.rds`.

**Step 1 — ages become entry years.** A child aged $a$ in the mid-2022
estimates enters Year 7 in $2022 + (11 - a)$. Ages 0 to 11 therefore give
entry years 2022 to 2033, every one of them children ONS has already
counted rather than births anyone has projected.

**Step 2 — two extrapolated years.** Children entering in 2034 and 2035
were not born when the mid-2022 estimate was taken. They are a linear
continuation of the five most recent observed cohorts, 2029 to 2033, and
are flagged as extrapolated wherever they appear.

**Step 3 — resident children become state-sector demand.** Not every
resident child takes a state Year 7 place: some go to independent
schools, some leave the city. A single constant carries that:

$$\text{state share} \;=\; \frac{\text{Year 7 offers made, 2024}}{\text{resident children, 2024 entry}} \;=\; `r sprintf("%.3f", STATE_SHARE)`$$

taken from the council's published offer total against the ONS cohort of
the same year.

**Step 4 — a ward is divided between its zones.** ONS publishes the age
detail at ward level, so each ward's children are shared out between the
zones in it. The weight for a zone is its LSOA's age-11 population share,
divided between the zones that LSOA is cut into by their postcode counts:

$$u_i \;=\; \underbrace{p_{\ell(i)}}_{\text{LSOA age-11 share}} \times
\underbrace{\frac{n_i}{\sum_{k \in \ell(i)} n_k}}_{\text{postcodes in this zone}},
\qquad
O_i^{\,2026} \;=\; \text{ward children}_{w(i)} \times \frac{u_i}{\sum_{k \in w(i)} u_k} \times \text{state share}$$

where $\ell(i)$ is the zone's LSOA, $w(i)$ its ward and $n_i$ its
postcode count. `r fmt_n(sum(!is.na(z$prop)))` of the `r fmt_n(nrow(z))`
zones use the ONS age share; the `r fmt_n(sum(is.na(z$prop)))` in the
expansion area outside the city fall back to postcode counts alone.

::: {.callout-note appearance="simple"}
## Why not divide a ward by postcode count

Splitting a ward's children by how many postcodes each LSOA holds assumes
every postcode contains the same number of eleven-year-olds. Family
housing, student housing and retirement housing differ enormously on
that, and the difference runs along exactly the lines catchment
boundaries follow. An earlier version of this work used postcode counts
between LSOAs, and the deprivation contrast between the two halves of a
paired catchment was almost entirely smoothed away. Postcode counts do
the narrower job above — dividing one LSOA between its own fragments,
where nothing finer is published.
:::

::: {.callout-important}
## Corrected: a split LSOA used to be counted twice

Until this version, each zone of a split LSOA inherited the **whole**
LSOA's age-11 share as its weight. Ward and city totals were unaffected,
because the denominator grew too, but inside a ward a split LSOA took
about 1.7 to 1.9 times the share its population implied and the unsplit
LSOAs around it took correspondingly less. The splits sit exactly on the
catchment boundaries, so the error was concentrated in the neighbourhoods
these questions turn on — Whitehawk and Kemptown among them.

The weight is now divided between a split LSOA's zones by their postcode
counts, as in the formula above. The table below is the check that used

### Where the city totals come from {#sec-oi-provenance}

Two different city-wide totals appear in this work, and they have
different provenance. It is worth being explicit about which is which.

**Offers actually made** are the council's own published figures. Its
[allocation factsheets](https://www.brighton-hove.gov.uk/schools-and-learning/school-admissions)
are issued after each March allocation round, and the series from 2010 to
2026 was transcribed school by school in the earlier
[Brighton and Hove allocations](https://adamdennett.github.io/BH_Schools_2/brighton_and_hove_allocations.html)
analysis. This bundle does not retype them: `public/R/01a_factsheet_panel.R`
re-extracts the year-by-year tables from that document, so every offer,
preference and admission number here traces back to a published council
factsheet.

**Resident children** are ONS, as set out in @sec-oi-sources. They are a
different quantity from offers: a resident child may go to an independent
school, or out of the city.

**The model's city total is the first anchored on the second.** The
state-sector share of `r sprintf("%.3f", STATE_SHARE)` is the council's
published 2024 offer total divided by the ONS resident cohort for the same
entry year, so the model's demand is ONS children scaled to the level the
council's own offers imply. It is not a free-floating estimate.

### Which projection this is, and the one beside it {#sec-oi-projection}

Two projections of the city's Year 7 cohort appear in this work. They are
built from different data and they are used for different things, so it
is worth being plain about which drives the model.

**The one the slider uses** is the ONS estimate carried forward: the
children counted in the mid-2022 small-area estimates, aged forward to
their Year 7 entry year (§2.4, steps 1 and 2), and multiplied by the
state-sector share. It is used because it is the only one that exists at
**ward** level, and the model needs the geography as well as the total —
$O_i$ is a ward figure divided between zones.

**The one beside it** is built from children already in the city's
primary schools: Reception offers made seven years earlier, multiplied by
the share of a Reception cohort that reappears as a Year 7 offer. That
ratio is measured rather than assumed, from the council's own published
factsheets, and it has been strikingly steady —
`r fmt_pct(100 * rc$survival_recent, 1)` across the five most recent
cohorts, with a standard deviation of
`r fmt_pct(100 * rc$survival_sd, 1)`. The method is set out in full in
[how our projection method works](https://adamdennett.github.io/BH_Schools_2/brighton_and_hove_allocations.html#how-our-projection-method-works),
and section 3 of the strategic view uses it as its headline projection
because it rests on children already registered in a school rather than
on a population estimate.

catchment totals year by year compares two different claims rather than
two estimates of one thing.

# $W_j$: how attractive each school is {#sec-wj}

## What it is

$W_j$ is the pull of school $j$ on a family, **net of where the school
stands**. It is dimensionless, scaled so the city average is 1, and it
enters the model multiplicatively: doubling $W_j$ doubles the numerator
of every flow into $j$ before distance, catchment and competition have
their say. It also enters the competing-destinations term, because a
school's rivals count for more when they are schools families want.

It is the hardest term in the model to pin down, because everything
observable about a school is entangled with where it is. A school with
high attainment may be sought after because it teaches well, or because
it sits where better-off families already live.

## Two layers: the open specifications, and the fitted values

**Layer one — the open specifications.** The open bundle carries five
candidate measures of attractiveness, so that a conclusion can be tested
across all of them rather than resting on one.

The central one is **weighted preferences per place**: the council's
published factsheets, pooled over the most recent rounds, counting first,
second and third preferences with a geometric discount of 0.5 per rank,
divided by the admission number. The paired catchments are why: a family
in Stringer / Varndean or Hove Park / Blatchington Mill must rank two
local schools against each other, so a first-preference count reads a
school families put second as one nobody wanted.

**Layer two — the fitted values the simulator uses.** The full model does
not take any of those five as given. It **balances** $W_j$ so that the
model reproduces the share of the city's first preferences each school
actually receives, using the council's catchment-level preference table
from its evidence to the Schools Adjudicator. The catchment terms
$\gamma_h$ are fitted in the same loop (§4, to follow), because the two
trade off against each other.

$$W_j \;\leftarrow\; W_j \left(\frac{\text{target share}_j}{\text{modelled share}_j}\right)^{0.8},
\qquad \text{repeat until } \max_j |\text{modelled} - \text{target}| < 10^{-6}$$

starting from the weighted-preference values, with the result rescaled to
a city mean of 1. The exponent 0.8 is damping: it stops the balancing
oscillating around the target. This is the same device as the balancing
factor $A_i$, applied to the other margin of the matrix — $A_i$ makes
every neighbourhood place all its children, and this makes every school
receive the share of first preferences it is observed to receive.

## How the model handles this, and what it cannot do

**What it does.** It fixes attractiveness at its recent value and gives
you a slider to move it. Every scenario in the strategic view that says
"a school half as attractive again" is that slider, and §3.6 converts a
slider position into points of Attainment 8 so the size of the
assumption is legible rather than abstract.

**What it does not do.** Attractiveness never responds to anything the
model itself produces. A school whose roll falls does not become less
wanted in the next year of the run; a school whose intake changes does
not see its results, and therefore its reputation, move with it. The
feedback loop the strategic view describes — a more disadvantaged intake,
lower headline scores, fewer preferences, a more disadvantaged intake
still — is **outside** this model. Where that loop is real, a run over ten
years understates how far apart schools drift.

That is the clearest case in this document for making time explicit
rather than implicit: $O_i$ already carries a year, and the natural next
step would be to let $W_j$ carry one too.
:::

## Why there is no exponent on $W_j$ {#sec-wj-alpha}

The general form of a production-constrained model carries a power on
attractiveness as well as on cost:

$$T_{ij} \;=\; A_i\, O_i\, W_j^{\alpha}\, c_{ij}^{-\beta}$$

Here $\alpha$ is absent, which is the same as fixing it at 1. That is a
deliberate choice and it follows from what $W_j$ is.

**A fitted quantity does not need a parameter to scale it.** $W_j$ is not
a proxy standing in for attractiveness; it *is* attractiveness, balanced
until the model reproduces each school's share of the city's first
preferences. An exponent on it would be a second free parameter doing the
same job as the balancing, and the two would trade off against each other:
any $\alpha$ can be absorbed by re-balancing $W$, because
$(W^{1/\alpha})^{\alpha} = W$. Fitting both would be fitting the same
thing twice, and the pair would not be identified.

**It would be needed if $W_j$ were a proxy.** Suppose attractiveness were
taken crudely as the number of pupils on roll, or as the admission
number, or as Attainment 8 — all of which this work carries as
alternative specifications (§3.2). None of those *is* pull; each is a
measurable thing believed to move with it, and there is no reason the
relationship should be one-for-one. A school twice the size is not
necessarily twice as wanted. An exponent would then be exactly the right
device: $\alpha$ fitted to observed flows would say how strongly demand
responds to the proxy, and $\alpha = 1$ would be a finding rather than an
assumption. In the classic retail application that is precisely what the
exponent on floorspace does.

So the rule is: **balance it, or scale it, but not both.** This model
balances. A version of it built on roll or on published attainment, which
is what anyone without the council's preference table would have to do,
should carry an $\alpha$ and fit it.

## What the attractiveness slider does

Each school's slider multiplies its fitted $W_j$, from
`r sprintf("%.2f", 0.25)` to `r sprintf("%.0f", 4)` times. Nothing else
changes: the same journey times, the same catchment terms, the same
admission numbers. Three details are worth knowing.

- **The multiplier is applied to the fitted value**, not to weighted
  preferences, so a slider at 1 reproduces the model exactly.
- **The out-of-city schools are not touched by it.** Priory, Peacehaven,
  Seahaven and Seaford Head carry their own fitted attractiveness and a
  decay on straight-line distance, and the sliders do not reach them.
- **CoMArt has no preferences**, because it does not exist. When it is
  opened, it borrows the attractiveness of
  `r inp$comart$w_from_short` as its starting value, and its slider moves
  from there.

## Turning a multiplier into something meaningful

A slider position is not a quantity anyone outside a model can judge, so
the app converts it to the one published number families appear to
respond to. Across the ten city schools, the fitted attractiveness is
log-linear in Attainment 8:

$$\log W_j = a + b \cdot \text{Attainment 8}_j,
\qquad b = `r sprintf("%.4f", inp$attain$slope)`,
\quad R^2 = `r sprintf("%.2f", inp$attain$r2)`,
\quad n = `r inp$attain$n`$$

so a point of Attainment 8 is worth about
`r fmt_pct(100 * inp$attain$per_point, 0)` more attractiveness, and a
multiplier $m$ inverts to $\log(m) / b$ points. That is how the simulator
answers "how good would this school have to be to fill?" — and it is an
association, not a lever: Attainment 8 is itself largely a description of
who a school admits.

## What to be careful about

- **Ten observations.** Every statement about *why* a school is
  attractive rests on ten schools. The balancing is an accounting
  identity across those ten; the Attainment 8 fit is a description of
  them, not evidence about causes.
- **Attractiveness and location are only partly separated.** The
  balancing asks what pull each school needs *given* where it stands and
  how far families will travel, which is a real separation — but it
  inherits whatever $\beta$ and $\gamma_h$ are set to. If distance decay
  were weaker, the fitted $W_j$ would be different.
- **The faith schools have no catchment**, so nothing in the model
  restricts who may choose them. Their fitted attractiveness absorbs the
  religious criteria that really ration those places, which is why they
  carry high values.
- **Peacehaven's preferences are not published** by the council, so both
  preference measures are filled at the lowest observed rate in the city.
  That is an assumption, and it flatters Longhill: a weak Peacehaven
  keeps eastern children in the city.
- **$W_j$ does not move with time.** A school that improves, or a new
  building, is a slider position rather than anything the model
  generates. Whether attractiveness should respond to a school's roll,
  its intake or its results is exactly the dynamic question §2.7 flags.

2. Should the balancing target first preferences, as now, or all three

3. Should a school's attractiveness respond to its own intake over time,

   does not contain?

## What it is

$c_{ij}$ is the **generalised cost** of getting from neighbourhood $i$ to
school $j$: minutes of walking and bus, door to door, on a weekday
morning. It is not a straight-line distance, and it is not a car journey.
It enters the model as $c_{ij}^{-\beta}$, so it is the term that makes a
school ten minutes away far more likely to be chosen than one an hour
away — and the exponent $\beta$ that governs how fast that falls away is
its own section, to follow.

The city has `r fmt_n(nrow(inp$cost$now))` zone-school pairs in the
simulator's cost table, spanning
`r sprintf("%.0f", min(inp$cost$now$cij))` to
`r sprintf("%.0f", max(inp$cost$now$cij))` minutes.

## Where the numbers come from

Every journey is routed with [`r5r`](https://ipeagit.github.io/r5r/) over
two networks: the street network from
[OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright), and the bus
timetable as a [GTFS feed](https://data.bus-data.dft.gov.uk/) from the
Department for Transport's Bus Open Data Service. The build is
`public/R/00b_build_travel_matrix.R` in the open bundle.

The departure date is chosen rather than assumed: the build counts the

lighter than a normal school day.

## From postcodes to zones

The matrix is routed **postcode to school**. A zone's cost is the mean
over the live postcodes in it:

$$c_{ij} \;=\; \frac{1}{|P_i|}\sum_{p \in P_i} c_{pj}$$

where $P_i$ is the set of postcodes in zone $i$. Because a zone is an
LSOA cut by catchment (§2.2), the two halves of a split LSOA get their
own journey times — which is the point of cutting them.

## Two corrections the raw routing needs

**Walking is always available.** Where the bus network is awkward the
router returns a poor itinerary: about a fifth of pairs came back slower
than simply walking the distance, in one case 99 minutes for 4 km. That
is a property of the router, not of the city, so every journey is capped
at the time it would take to walk:

$$c_{ij} \;=\; \min\!\left(c^{\text{routed}}_{ij},\;
\frac{d_{ij} \times `r sprintf("%.1f", acc$walk_fallback$circuity)`}{`r sprintf("%.2f", acc$walk_fallback$kmh)` \text{ km/h}} \times 60\right)$$

The walking pace comes from a systematic review of active school travel:
children's mean walking trip is
`r fmt_n(acc$walk_fallback$review$dist_m)` m in
`r sprintf("%.1f", acc$walk_fallback$review$time_min)` minutes, or
`r sprintf("%.2f", acc$walk_fallback$kmh)` km/h, and the circuity factor
of `r sprintf("%.1f", acc$walk_fallback$circuity)` turns straight-line
distance into street distance. The cap binds on
`r fmt_pct(100 * mean(costs$walk_capped), 0)` of pairs. **It is a ceiling
on modelled time, not a claim that children walk that far** — the same
review finds they rarely walk beyond 1.8 km.

**A distance model fills the gaps.** Where the matrix has no routed value
at all, a fitted model of time on straight-line distance stands in. It
reaches `r fmt_pct(100 - 100 * mean(costs$routed), 1)` of pairs in the
city, so almost everything here is routed rather than predicted.

## What to be careful about

- **One departure, one day.** Every journey is a single weekday morning
  in the timetable as it stood when the matrix was built. Term-time
  variation, a missed connection and a cancelled bus are all outside it.
- **A mean over postcodes.** A zone's cost is the average of the
  postcodes in it, so a neighbourhood with one badly connected corner
  shows as slightly worse rather than showing both.
- **No cars, no lifts, no walking buses.** The model prices the journey a
  child could make on foot and by bus. A family with a car makes a
  different journey, and that is one reason the east's figures should be
  read as a ceiling on difficulty rather than a description of it.
- **Time is symmetric here.** One morning journey stands for the return
  as well, and no allowance is made for after-school travel.
- **The cap is generous at distance.** A 6 km walk is not a real school
  journey, but capping at walking time is deliberately crude: it removes
  router artefacts without inventing a bus that does not run.

## Questions this raises

1. Should the zone cost be the mean over its postcodes, or the
   child-weighted mean, or the best available journey rather than the
   average one?
2. Should journeys be routed at more than one departure time, so that a
   thin early timetable is visible?
3. Should the out-of-city schools be routed rather than measured on
   straight-line distance, if a feed covering East Sussex services were
   used?

# $\beta$: how sharply distance deters {#sec-beta}

```{r beta-setup}
#| include: false

BETA <- inp$params$beta
ext <- cal$outside
```

## What it is

$\beta$ is the exponent on journey time. It converts the minutes of §4
into a pull:

$$\text{pull of school } j \text{ on neighbourhood } i \;\propto\; c_{ij}^{-\beta}$$

A larger $\beta$ means families discount distance more sharply. It is a
single number for the whole city, fixed at **`r sprintf("%.1f", BETA)`**,
and it is the same in every run of the simulator: no slider moves it.

The form matters as much as the value. This is a **power** decay, not an
exponential one: doubling the journey divides the pull by
$2^{`r sprintf("%.1f", BETA)`} \approx `r sprintf("%.1f", 2^BETA)`$,
whatever the starting point. That is the standard form in spatial
interaction modelling, and it behaves sensibly over the range of journeys
a city this size contains.

## Where the value comes from, and what it is not

**It is a judgement, not an estimate.** Fitting $\beta$ properly needs
observed flows — how many children actually travel from each
neighbourhood to each school — and the council has not released them. A
catchment-level preference table identifies the catchment terms (§6, to
follow) but not distance decay, because within a catchment every family
is at a different distance and the table does not say who is where.

So `r sprintf("%.1f", BETA)` is a central value chosen for a system of
this kind: journeys are short, the alternatives sit close together, and
it is squarely inside the range this literature reports for school
choice. The open model does not rely on it being right. It runs
everything across `r length(env$betas)` values from
`r sprintf("%.1f", min(env$betas))` to `r sprintf("%.1f", max(env$betas))`
and reports which conclusions survive the whole range.

::: {.callout-note appearance="simple"}
## What would pin it down

A Poisson regression of flows on log journey time with an origin fixed
effect returns $\beta$ with a standard error, from records the admissions
process already produces. It is half a day's work on data the council
holds. Every band in the open model that comes from sweeping $\beta$
would become a number with an interval around it.
:::

## A second decay, on a different scale

The four schools outside the city do not use $\beta$ at all. They are
chosen on straight-line kilometres with a decay of their own, fitted to
the published 2024 destinations:

$$\text{pull of an out-of-city school} \;=\; W^{\text{ext}}_j \; d_{ij}^{-`r sprintf("%.1f", ext$decay)`}$$

`r sprintf("%.1f", ext$decay)` is far steeper than
`r sprintf("%.1f", BETA)`, and the two are not comparable: this one is on
kilometres rather than minutes, and it is doing a different job. It is
absorbing everything that stops a Brighton family choosing a school in
another authority — the admissions arrangements of a different council,
the absence of a school bus, the sheer unfamiliarity — and compressing it
into the one dimension the fit has. It reproduces the published pattern,
which is a low bar for four schools and one year of data, and it should
not be read as a behavioural estimate at all.

## What to be careful about

- **One exponent for the whole city.** Families differ in how far they
  will travel, and the literature is consistent that the willingness
  varies with income and with whether a car is available. A single
  $\beta$ averages over that, and it is the assumption most likely to
  matter for the equity questions in the strategic view.
- **Everything else is fitted *given* this value.** The attractiveness
  balancing (§3) and the catchment terms (§6) are estimated with $\beta$
  held at `r sprintf("%.1f", BETA)`. A different decay would give
  different fitted values for both, which is why the sweep re-runs the
  whole model rather than swapping the exponent at the end.
- **Time, not distance, and not cost.** A journey that is quick but
  requires two buses and a fare is treated as a short journey.
- **The floor at short journeys.** A power decay rises steeply as time
  approaches zero, so the very shortest journeys carry a lot of weight.
  The routed minimum in the table is
  `r sprintf("%.0f", min(costs$cij))` minutes, which keeps it in bounds.

## Questions this raises

1. Should $\beta$ vary between groups of families — by deprivation, or by
   whether a catchment has one school or two — rather than being one
   number for the city?
2. Is the power form right, or would an exponential decay fit the pattern
   of Brighton's journeys better? Both are standard; they differ most at
   the long-journey end, which is where the eastern questions sit.
3. Should the out-of-city decay be re-fitted once more than one year of
   destination data is available, or replaced by a proper choice model
   for schools in another authority?
# $\gamma_h$: how much living in the catchment counts {#sec-gamma}

```{r gamma-setup}
#| include: false
gam <- cal$gamma
gam_lab <- setNames(unname(CATCH_LABELS[CATCH_FROM_MODEL[names(gam)]]), names(gam))
r_now <- run_sim(inp, year = 2026, rules = list(rule = "priorities"))
```

## What it is

$\gamma_h$ is the bonus a school gets for being **in the family's own
catchment**. It sits in the exponent, so it multiplies a school's pull:

$$\text{pull} \;\propto\; W_j\, c_{ij}^{-\beta} \times
\begin{cases} e^{\gamma_{h(i)}} & \text{if } j \text{ is a school of } i\text{'s catchment}\\ 1 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

The subscript matters: $\gamma$ is indexed by **$h(i)$, the catchment the
family lives in**, not by the school. Stringer / Varndean families are
modelled as following their map more closely than Brighton Aldridge's
families follow theirs, and that is a statement about families rather
than about schools.

::: {.callout-important}
## This is about what families name, not about who gets admitted

Admission priority is a separate stage of the model (§8, to follow):
when a school is oversubscribed the council's criteria decide who is
offered a place, and living in the catchment is one of them. $\gamma_h$
is doing something different and earlier. It says how much more likely a
family is to **want** a school because it is the one their map points
them to — through habit, siblings, the school bus, or simply reading the
catchment as a statement about where their child belongs.

The distinction is why the strategic view can find that catchment
priority allocates nothing at an undersubscribed school while the
catchment still matters a great deal there: nobody is turned away, but
the map still shapes what families ask for.
:::

## How they are fitted

The council's evidence to the Schools Adjudicator includes a
catchment-level preference table: how many first, second and third
preferences each catchment's children gave each school, pooled over
`r length(mt$calibrated$rounds)` rounds. That is a flow matrix — coarse,
but a matrix — and it is enough to fit one term per catchment.

The loop is:

1. Take the current catchment terms and **balance $W_j$** so the model
   reproduces each school's share of the city's first preferences (§3).
2. For one catchment, try every $\gamma$ on a grid from
   `r sprintf("%.0f", 0)` to `r sprintf("%.0f", 4)` in steps of 0.1,
   re-balancing $W_j$ at each step, and keep the value that minimises the
   **Poisson deviance** between the modelled and observed shares in that
   table.
3. Repeat for each catchment, then sweep again until nothing moves.

The deviance is the right loss here because the cells are counts of
preferences: it weights a cell by how many families it contains, so a

## What the slider does

The simulator's catchment slider multiplies **every** fitted term by the
same factor, from 0 to 2. At 0 the map stops mattering to what families
name and the model becomes distance and attractiveness alone; at 2 every
catchment's attachment doubles in the exponent, which is a very strong
map indeed. At 1 it uses the fitted values.

That single multiplier is deliberately blunt. It answers "what if
families follow their catchments more, or less, than they do now?"
without pretending to know which catchment would change.

## Where it sits in the matrix

Unlike $O_i$ and $W_j$, the catchment term does not live in a margin. It
is a **mask over the interior**: for each neighbourhood, the cells of
schools in its own catchment are multiplied by $e^{\gamma_h}$ and the
rest are left alone.

## What to be careful about

::: {.callout-warning}
## The assumption every redraw result depends on

The terms are fitted on the map families have lived with, and then
**carried unchanged to any other map**. A redraw in the simulator moves
which schools count as in catchment; it does not change how strongly a
family follows the line.

That is the single most consequential assumption in this work. If
families follow a new boundary less closely than the one they know —
which the strategic view finds some evidence for in what happened after
2024 — then every redraw result here is too optimistic about how many
children the redraw moves. The strategic view reports the halved-term
version of the relocation runs for exactly this reason.
:::

- **The term is fitted on the pre-2024 map**, because that is the map the
  preference table covers.
- **It bundles several mechanisms.** Siblings already at the school, the
  free bus, habit, and the sense that the catchment school is *the*
  school all end up in one number, because the table cannot separate
  them.
- **It is a home-catchment term, not a school term.** A school in two
  catchments — none exist today, but CoMArt opening would create one —
  takes each catchment's own strength.
- **Paired catchments are a single object here.** A Stringer / Varndean
  family gets the bonus on both schools, and which of the two they prefer
  is left to $W_j$, distance, and the exclusivity populations.

## Questions this raises

1. Should the term decay when a boundary changes — a family newly placed
   in a catchment following it less closely than one that has been in it
   for years?
2. Should it vary with how many schools a catchment contains, rather than
   being one number per catchment?
3. Would first preferences by small area, before and after the 2024
   change, let the normative half of this be separated from the
   procedural half?
